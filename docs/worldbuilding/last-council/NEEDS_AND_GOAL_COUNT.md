# Needs, bargaining and goal count

[Workbook](README.md) · [Houses](HOUSES.md) · [Goals](GOALS.md) · [Rough map](MAP.md)

> **Design analysis · 23 November 2026.** The configurable goal count is requested.
> Needs are being reconsidered, not approved or implemented.

## The gap in the current design

An offer is useful only when someone has a reason to value it. Knowing that
Harrowmere has spare wheat does not tell another player why their House should
pay for it, exchange a favour or support Harrowmere's motion.

The revised public identities make suppliers easier to recognise. They do not
create demand. Two political goals give players more to pursue, but neither
goal is guaranteed to make grain, ships or quarry stone relevant.

Private information currently does two different jobs. A failed order or debt
already gives its owner a problem. Spare stock gives its owner an opportunity,
which may remain unused. That difference matters more than whether an entry
is called a secret or a need.

## The options

| Approach | What it adds | Main cost |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the current draw | Public identities, private facts and political goals create negotiations. Resource offers may go unused. | A pure supply entry can contribute little to a particular evening. |
| Add broad, optional needs | Some Houses receive an immediate concern with several possible responses; others have no urgent need. | More information to read and one more part of the draw to check. |
| Add a full resource-needs system | Houses require specified quantities of food, fuel, weapons or transport. | Scarcity, capacity, absent suppliers and fulfilment become an economic game to manage. |
| Replace pure supply entries | Every House entry centres on a political opportunity, vulnerability or dispute without relying on a buyer. | Resource bargaining becomes less important to House identity and play. |

**My recommendation:** if resource trading remains an important part of the
experience, try broad, optional needs in a paper test. Do not build a full
resource ledger. If we decide against needs and want every House entry to
carry its own political stake, revise the pure supply entries. We can also
accept occasional unused offers. Adding more goals alone will not reliably
create demand for every resource.

A shared political question can create demand without individual needs:
funding border defence, for example, makes weapons and transport relevant.
That question must be part of the visible situation. We should not assume
that players know it exists or make the Chancellor invent a buyer whenever
an offer fails.

This is a proposed next design step, not a decision to add another mechanic.

## What a lightweight need could mean

A need gives the player a reason to act and explains what is at stake. It
leaves the response open.

| Example private concern | Possible routes |
|---|---|
| Your winter grain deliveries are no longer secured. Paying the higher open-market price would consume most of the income from your estates. | Negotiate supplies, obtain credit, combine transport with another House or seek a Crown purchasing agreement. |
| Your mounted troops need replacement horses before the border patrols begin. Buying them individually would leave the patrols short. | Buy horses, arrange a shared patrol, finance another House's contribution or seek a different border policy. |
| Your main trade route will close before winter. The loss threatens a large share of your market income. | Find carriers, fund repairs, negotiate another route or change the Crown's delivery requirements. |
| Your existing supplies and contracts cover the coming winter. | No immediate material need. Pursue your political goals or negotiate from a stronger position. |

These would be facts in the fiction. There would be no automatic hunger
penalty, troop counter, purchase checklist or success score. An agreement
can help a player feel secure without the mod judging their solution.

The examples are alternatives, not a new pool or additional cards already
assigned to the Houses. We would write that pool only after choosing this
direction.

Not every House should need wheat, and not every resource should have a
guaranteed buyer. Refusing an offer must remain a valid choice. Competition
for a limited supply can be interesting; forcing every seller into a
prearranged deal is not necessary.

## How the information fits together

| Part | Its job |
|---|---|
| Public House identity | Establishes what the House is known for and whom it is sensible to approach. |
| Private House entry | Establishes its actual capacity, hidden weakness, authority or business position this time. |
| General secret | Adds a separate vulnerability, hidden asset or compromising history. |
| Goals | Describe the political outcomes or principles the player wants to pursue. |
| Optional need | Adds an immediate pressure when the other material leaves the player without one. |

A House with a private entry about an unpaid weapons order already has a
pressure. Giving it a second unrelated shortage can bury its political goals
under errands. I would normally give **at most one additional need**, and
allow no additional need when the House entry already supplies a strong
problem.

Goals should stay independently drawn. A House seeking peace can still need
weapons for defence. A character with a shameful secret can sincerely want
fairer government. Those tensions create choices. A need saying that the
House has no wheat would, however, contradict private information saying
its winter wheat is already secured. That requires a different need, not
an explanation invented after the draw.

Needs should not reveal other Houses' private entries. A player may know
that they need grain without knowing whether Harrowmere has any to spare.
Harrowmere can bargain, conceal its position or lie. Random knowledge of
secrets remains separate: zero, one or several other Houses may learn a given
secret, and its owner does not learn who knows it. The owner always receives
its own private facts.

## Balancing against the Houses actually playing

Use the assigned, participating cast when papers are issued. Do not assume
that an unassigned House is available as a negotiating partner, or redraw
everyone's information when somebody briefly disconnects.

The check should ask **“Does this pressure offer a plausible action in this
cast?”**, rather than “Have we assigned its matching supplier?”

1. Draw the House information, general secrets and eligible goals independently.
2. Consider what the participating Houses can plausibly offer. Public grain
   production is a lead; a private entry may show that all its stock is already
   promised or cannot be delivered.
3. If optional needs are enabled, exclude literal contradictions and pressures
   with no plausible opening in this cast and setting. Redraw the proposed
   need, not someone else's secret to manufacture a solution.
4. Keep several routes open where possible: a supplier, financing, a route
   agreement or a Council decision. Avoid a chain in which one absent player
   prevents everyone else from acting.
5. Commit the result. Reconnects, discoveries and failed negotiations do not
   change the facts already given to players.

Existing foreign merchants or Crown decisions can provide another route,
but they should not become an unlimited free fallback. An outside purchase
might cost much more, require an unpopular policy or arrive too late for
the original plan. Those conditions need to be stated in the fiction.

Do not guarantee that enough supplies exist for every promise. Shortages,
overpromising and refusing to cooperate can generate play. The preparation
must provide meaningful attempts, not guaranteed satisfaction.

It must also be possible for a particular House to have no urgent material
need. If the entire cast draws that result, resource trading may simply not
be important that evening. We should accept that possibility or openly
choose a minimum level of pressure as a setup rule; we should not silently
rig the secret draw to guarantee buyers.

## A five-House example

Suppose the cast is Harrowmere, Durnholt, Selwyn, Caldren and Morcant:

- **Harrowmere** has imported wheat, but the seller still owns it until the
  remaining bill is paid.
- **Durnholt** has stone to deliver, but its carrier failed and its royal land
  grant is at risk.
- **Selwyn** can place orders with winter carriers.
- **Caldren** has money available to lend.
- **Morcant** can equip armies with weapons.

Durnholt and Harrowmere already have strong pressures. They can approach
Selwyn or Caldren, propose a joint Crown contract or renegotiate their
obligations. Selwyn and Caldren do not have to need weapons to participate.

Morcant's supply remains the uncertain case. A border-defence debate might
give it a buyer. Without such a debate or a relevant private concern,
weapons may not matter this evening. An optional need elsewhere could create
demand, but it should not always do so. If we want every House entry to
matter without that uncertainty, Morcant's pure supply entry needs its own
political stake instead.

This is why I would avoid **“secret A requires secret B.”** It makes the draw
look random while quietly preparing the relationships in advance.

## Goal count: requested in-game setting

Record **Goals per House** as an editable scenario setting, with **2 as the
default**. Each House draws that many different eligible goals. Another
House may draw the same goals.

The number is setup configuration, not a `${number.*}` placeholder embedded
in a book. One numeric setting is sufficient; independently rolling a
different count for every House is a separate design choice, not assumed
here.

Check the requested count against each House's eligible pool before issuing
papers. If it cannot be supplied, explain the problem before committing the
draw. Do not silently issue fewer goals or repeat one within the same House.

Keep the chosen count with the prepared scenario. After papers are issued,
changing that setting should not redistribute an existing cast's goals.
This draft interprets "per House" as sharing those goals if several characters
represent one House. That is a design assumption for this proposal. Personal
ambitions written by players remain their own.

## What to test before deciding on needs

Try a small cast with the same public identities and two goals per House:
first without additional needs, then with some broad needs and some settled
Houses. Include a seller whose normal buyer is absent and a supplier whose
private entry limits its stock.

Look for whether offers lead to genuine negotiations, whether players can
recognise their stakes, and whether needs crowd out their political goals.
Also check whether a player can refuse a deal and still find something
meaningful to do. No scoring system is required for that review.

Neither version has been playtested with this revised content. The needs
direction remains open; the requested House identities, map and configurable
goal-count notes do not commit us to it.
