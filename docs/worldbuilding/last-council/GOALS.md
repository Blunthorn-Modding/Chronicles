# House goals: a pool of ambitions

> Worldbuilding draft. These are original, editable motivations for play; they are not implemented content.

Goals invite characters to pursue what matters to them, make compromises and find their own sense of accomplishment. They are not scored tasks or fixed end states. The organizer chooses how many to issue and checks cast, political state and compatibility with the selected House background. The common institutions and starting laws in [the setting](SETTING.md) are public knowledge. Suggestions are openings for roleplay, not prescribed routes. Where an author note calls for another House or realm, bind it to an available participant or realm before issue; other players decide their own loyalties and responses.

## Crown, influence and power

### G01 — Claim the Crown

Your family believes it can hold this uneven realm together better than another dynasty. A crown would also raise your name above every rival, and you are honest enough to know that both motives matter. Seek a path to candidacy, court votes, offer concessions or challenge rival claims. Think about what your House would surrender for the throne and what it would refuse to promise.

**Author-only eligibility:** An eligible participating candidate of your House, or a credible route to candidacy before the slate closes.

### G02 — A ruler who respects the provinces

The realm was assembled from provinces with different customs, and your family fears a ruler who treats those customs as mere obstacles. Find a candidate willing to respect local rights, and explore what assurances would be convincing. Public endorsements, a negotiated programme or a coalition of wary Houses could all serve your purpose, although a strong Crown has supporters too.

**Author-only eligibility:** A participating candidate, or a credible route to candidacy, before the slate closes.

### G03 — Patronage at court

Your House wants the ear of the next ruler. A favourable appointment, a trade licence or a trusted place in the royal household could turn influence into security for years. Approach a participating candidate and offer useful support, information or political companionship. Others may see a bargain for office as corruption, particularly if the price becomes public.

**Author-only eligibility:** A participating candidate, or a credible route to candidacy, before the slate closes.

### G04 — The power behind the throne

You would rather shape a ruler than wear the Crown yourself. A friendly sovereign might listen to your advice on appointments and policy, but dependence on one candidate is a dangerous wager. Sound out the contenders, draw allies around a programme and consider whether your influence should be openly acknowledged or quietly exercised.

**Author-only eligibility:** A participating candidate, or a credible route to candidacy, before the slate closes.

### G05 — A divided Crown

Your family distrusts concentrated authority. It wants a ruler who must listen to a broad Council, even when quick decisions would be easier. Defend provincial voices and court Houses that fear being excluded. You could support a candidate prepared to share power, or use the interregnum to establish political expectations that make an autocratic settlement harder to sell.

**Author-only eligibility:** A participating candidate, or a credible route to candidacy, before the slate closes.

### G06 — A strong hand

The vacant throne has shown how slowly many interests can move together. Your family wants the next ruler to exercise clear authority in emergencies and to command a coherent policy. Seek supporters who value decisive leadership, but listen to those who fear arbitrary rule. You might trade safeguards for their backing or persuade a candidate to champion your vision.

**Author-only eligibility:** A participating candidate, or a credible route to candidacy, before the slate closes.

### G07 — Never be taken for granted

Your House has no wish to be a candidate's obedient vote. Make your support valuable enough that contenders must listen to your priorities, whether those concern local rights, public works or a place at court. Invite competing offers, build a bloc or hold your endorsement until the debate has substance. Delay may bring leverage, but it can also cost friends.

**Author-only eligibility:** A participating candidate, or a credible route to candidacy, before the slate closes.

### G08 — Influence without a crown

The succession may dominate the palace, but government continues during the vacancy. Your family wants to leave its mark on the settlement regardless of who wins. Bring an issue you care about before the Council, gather a coalition and explore how to bind political commitments to a wider programme. A candidate might become your ally or try to claim your achievement.

## Family security and status

### G09 — Independence at any price

Your family fears becoming dependent on one richer House or a central court for its position. Protect room to bargain by building several relationships and opposing arrangements that place all authority in one set of hands. This may require declining an attractive patron or accepting a smaller immediate gain. Decide whether independence is worth the opportunities it costs.

### G10 — A place among the old names

Wealth and influence do not always bring respect. Your family wants a recognised place among the realm's leading Houses, and resents the idea that precedence belongs only to age. Seek public recognition, an appointment or a partnership that puts your name beside established rivals. Consider whether to challenge their pretensions openly or make yourself indispensable to them.

### G11 — Defend the family name

Your House is determined that its standing will not be diminished in the settlement. Other delegations may see its claims differently, and you would rather answer criticism through your own conduct than let rivals define your family. Speak for its service, seek allies willing to acknowledge it, and decide how much pride you can put aside for a practical bargain.

### G12 — Protect a local privilege

Your family values the patchwork of local charters because it gives communities a voice beyond the court. Choose a local custom or privilege that matters to you and explain why it deserves protection. You might seek a confirmation, build a coalition with other defenders of local rights or negotiate a reform that preserves its essential purpose.

### G13 — Security through service

Your House wants its future standing to rest on useful public work, not merely an old title. A road, harbour, bridge or relief programme could give other Houses a reason to remember its contribution. Seek partners and argue for a commission, while considering whether the work serves the public or chiefly advertises your family.

### G14 — A refuge from uncertainty

The disputed succession makes even established families worry about tomorrow. Your House wants dependable relationships and a voice in the settlement, without becoming the client of a single patron. Look for mutual assurances, shared projects or political allies with different strengths. A promise offered too cheaply may later limit the choices you wanted to preserve.

### G15 — The inheritance of obligation

Your family believes that status should carry an obligation to protect those who depend on it. Use your influence to argue for a relief programme or safeguards for a vulnerable community. Other Houses may ask who will pay or accuse you of buying admiration. Decide whether to work quietly with partners or make your principle a public test of leadership.

### G16 — A seat at every table

Your House is tired of important bargains being made before it is invited into the room. Cultivate relationships across rival groups and insist on a voice when decisions affect your interests. You could offer mediation, useful support or public opposition to a closed bargain. Being welcomed by everyone may mean disappointing those who want a firm allegiance.

## Reform and privilege

### G17 — The cost of a closed market

Your family believes exclusive commercial privileges can enrich a few while leaving others dependent. Challenge any proposed monopoly and argue for a more open market or limited licences with public obligations. You may find allies among Houses excluded from a bargain, but a rival can reasonably argue that exclusive rights attract investment or fund public work.

### G18 — A profitable exception

Your House wants a limited commercial privilege of its own. An exclusive licence over a particular route or activity could reward political support and fund future ambitions, though rivals may see it as a private tax on everyone else. Negotiate its scope, offer a public benefit or persuade a candidate that your enterprise deserves protection.

### G19 — Roads for all

Under the Open Roads Ordinance, customary tolls are lawful and new tolls or increases need Council approval. Your family worries that the cost of movement could leave provincial communities isolated. Argue for restraint, a fairer arrangement or public support for roads. A House seeking toll revenue may answer that somebody must pay for their upkeep.

### G20 — Let the toll pay for the road

A useful bridge or road needs support, and your House favours letting its users contribute. The Open Roads Ordinance permits customary tolls and requires Council approval for a new toll or increase. Propose a location and purpose, negotiate limits or exemptions, and make the case to those who fear that even a modest charge will fall hardest on ordinary travellers.

### G21 — A fairer charter

The realm's local charters protect varied privileges, but a privilege that once served a town may also exclude its neighbours. Your family wants to examine and improve such arrangements. Pick a plausible question for debate and seek reform, a compromise or a review. Be prepared to explain why local autonomy should yield to your idea of fairness.

### G22 — Custom against uniformity

A single tidy rule can erase the bargains that brought provinces into the realm. Your House wants to defend local variation even when reformers promise efficiency or equal treatment. Listen for proposals that threaten a custom and try to build a coalition around the value of particular rights. Decide which reforms you could accept without surrendering the principle.

### G23 — Gambling without shame

Public gambling establishments require a licence, while household games and private wagers are already lawful. Your family thinks respectable public gambling should be treated as ordinary business, not a mark of moral disgrace. Argue for accessible licensing or a more tolerant public attitude. Critics may worry about exploitation, so consider what protections you would support.

### G24 — The price of an office

Paid licences, appointments and commercial advantages must be disclosed; concealed payment for official favour is an offence. Your family believes even disclosed purchases can make government look like a marketplace. Argue for limits, greater openness or a competing standard of merit. Others may defend patronage as a practical way to fund service and reward loyalty.

## Agreements, debts and concessions

### G25 — A bargain of equals

Your family wants a useful agreement with another participating House, but refuses to appear its junior partner. Find an issue where each party can offer something the other values: political support, a local project or a public commitment. Negotiate terms that respect both interests. A bargain that looks equal on paper may still leave one party owing more in practice.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G26 — A promise worth keeping

The Crown can register or guarantee a specified debt, grant or supply promise. Your family sees a chance to make cooperation more reliable by negotiating a new agreement and seeking recognition for it. Decide what you are prepared to pledge and what assurance you want in return. The other House is a free negotiator, not a pre-existing debtor.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G27 — Concessions for a cause

Your House has a policy it wants badly enough to bargain for it. Decide what the policy is, then explore whose support could advance it and what you might offer in return. A trading privilege, an endorsement or support for a local concern could all become currency. Consider where a useful concession becomes a betrayal of your own position.

### G28 — The debt of gratitude

Not every obligation is written in a ledger. Your family wants to cultivate political goodwill by helping another delegation with a present problem, then asking for support when your own interests arise. The help and the request must be discovered in conversation. You may earn a genuine ally, or reveal that generosity was also an investment.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G29 — A coalition with a price

Your family wants to assemble a coalition around a policy of your choosing. Each supporter may have a different price, and promises that satisfy one House can alarm another. Talk to potential allies, compare their demands and decide what you can honestly offer. A broad but fragile coalition may be less useful than a smaller, trusted partnership.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G30 — The unsold vote

Your House considers its Council voice a trust, not merchandise. Resist offers that would exchange your judgment for personal advantage, and encourage others to say publicly where their loyalties lie. This principle need not prevent compromise over policy. The difficult question is whether a reciprocal agreement serves the realm or merely disguises a sale.

### G31 — The art of the counteroffer

You expect other Houses to come seeking your support, and your family intends to make that interest profitable. Listen carefully, then ask for a concession that advances a distinct concern of your own. You could demand openness, a share of patronage or support for a project. Push too hard and your reputation for bargaining may become a reputation for unreliability.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G32 — A compact for the road

Your family wants to make travel and exchange more dependable through cooperation with another House. Choose a road, crossing or shared commercial concern during discussion, and consider a voluntary compact or a Council warrant. Each side may value the project differently; the interesting bargain is how much support each will offer and what it expects in return.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

## Foreign policy and conflict

### G33 — Credit across the water

Rheven is a maritime neighbour known for grain, shipping and credit. Your family values access to foreign credit as a way for Houses and merchants to avoid dependence on a single domestic patron. Argue for open, declared dealings and seek allies who share that interest. Suspicious rivals may ask whether financial independence at home creates influence from abroad.

**Author-only eligibility:** Rheven exists in the setup.

### G34 — The danger of foreign credit

Rheven can supply credit as well as goods and shipping. Your family fears that a Crown eager for funds could surrender political freedom for easy loans. Seek scrutiny of future royal borrowing or negotiate limits acceptable to supporters of trade. Consider whether a strict rule would protect the realm or leave it unable to respond to a real need.

**Author-only eligibility:** Rheven exists in the setup.

### G35 — Ships and open markets

Trade with Rheven is lawful, though imported goods and foreign commercial payments must be declared. Your House wants the sea route to remain open and legitimate traders treated without suspicion. Build support among Houses that value exchange, and discuss what transparency or safeguards might reassure critics who fear foreign leverage.

**Author-only eligibility:** Rheven exists in the setup.

### G36 — The cost of reliance

Rheven offers grain, shipping and credit, all useful to a realm whose needs cross provincial boundaries. Your House worries about depending too heavily on any one external route. Promote alternatives, shared transport or cautious purchasing while remaining open to lawful trade. Others may see your plan as prudent resilience or an expensive insult to a valuable neighbour.

**Author-only eligibility:** Rheven exists in the setup.

### G37 — A quiet Vask border

Vask shares land routes and border communities with the realm. Your family wants disputes handled through negotiation and restraint before they harden into conflict. Persuade others that a peaceful approach can coexist with vigilance, and consider what promises or preparations would make cautious Houses feel secure. Vask has made no offer and need not appear at the Council.

**Author-only eligibility:** Vask exists in the setup.

### G38 — Ready for the worst

The border with Vask connects communities and commerce, but it also gives either realm reason to worry about security. Your House favours credible defensive preparation and wants that concern taken seriously. Seek political support for a defensive programme, while weighing the risk that alarming rhetoric or excessive preparations could make a peaceful neighbour more suspicious.

**Author-only eligibility:** Vask exists in the setup.

### G39 — Neighbours before enemies

People and commerce cross the routes near Vask. Your family wants their legitimate connections protected when the Council discusses the border. Speak for practical access and lawful trade, or seek support for a policy that considers border communities. Others may ask how openness can be reconciled with security; the answer is yours to negotiate.

**Author-only eligibility:** Vask exists in the setup.

### G40 — Who speaks for the realm?

A concession offered abroad can bind the realm's reputation even when it benefits one faction at home. Your family wants foreign policy concerning Rheven or Vask discussed openly among the Houses before promises are made. Build support for shared scrutiny, but consider whether public debate might weaken a negotiator's flexibility or expose divisions to a neighbour.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least one of Rheven or Vask exists; choose an available realm before issue.

## Reputation, loyalty and ethics

### G41 — The indispensable mediator

Your family wants to be the House others approach when negotiations stall. Seek a dispute that emerges during play, listen to both sides and offer a compromise or a quiet meeting. You need not choose a winner, but neutrality may look like cowardice to those who want an ally. Decide when preserving access matters less than taking a stand.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least three participating Houses.

### G42 — Shield the smaller voices

Your family fears that a powerful coalition could settle the realm while weaker delegations are left with no meaningful say. Look for Houses whose concerns are being overlooked and help them speak or bargain together. No House begins as your client and no coalition is presumed hostile. Others may accuse you of gathering clients under the language of protection.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least three participating Houses.

### G43 — Loyalty with conditions

Your House values loyalty, but believes it should be earned through conduct rather than demanded by rank. Offer a candidate or political ally sincere support in return for a shared principle, and consider what would make you reconsider. Unconditional loyalists may call you unreliable; opportunists may think you too constrained by honour.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G44 — A reputation for candour

Your family wants to be known for saying difficult things openly, especially when a comfortable bargain hides a cost to others. Challenge vague promises and ask what their sponsors are prepared to give up. Candour can earn trust, but it can also humiliate a potential ally. Decide when a private warning would serve your principles better than a public challenge.

### G45 — Trust after stigma

Your family wants others to judge it by its present conduct rather than by a narrow public reputation. No particular scandal need be assumed: even lawful ambition or an unpopular trade can attract courtly contempt. Seek relationships across that divide, demonstrate reliability or confront the prejudice directly. You decide whether respect is worth changing how your House behaves.

### G46 — A favour for the forgotten

Your family believes political settlements too often overlook people who have no seat at the Council. Champion a relief programme, a local privilege or a more equitable public work and ask other Houses to share the burden. A rival may question whether your concern is sincere or whether the proposed remedy truly helps. Let the argument test your convictions.

### G47 — The useful rival

Your House wants a lasting working relationship with another delegation whose interests may differ from its own. Look for someone willing to disagree honestly and find a policy on which cooperation is possible. Public praise, a shared project or a carefully limited bargain might begin that relationship. Friendship across a divide can alarm allies who prefer clear camps.

**Author-only eligibility:** At least two participating Houses.

### G48 — A principle with a cost

Choose a principle your family will defend in this Council: local liberty, transparent patronage, relief, order or another concern grounded in the public setting. State why it matters and seek others who share it. If an attractive offer tests that principle, decide whether to compromise, refuse or redefine your position. Conviction should matter most when it has a price.

### G49 — Expose the bargain, not a secret

Your family dislikes political deals made without explaining their public consequences. When a proposal arises, ask its supporters to name who benefits, who pays and what they expect in return. You can pursue scrutiny without assuming anyone has committed a crime or possesses a particular secret. Others may argue that some negotiations need privacy to produce a workable compromise.

### G50 — Mercy and accountability

Your House believes a political settlement should leave room for a person to admit a mistake and return to lawful conduct. The Council can debate the scope of a future amnesty, for example for voluntary disclosure of an undisclosed paid favour. Seek a balance between mercy and deterrence, without assuming a particular offender exists. Others may see forgiveness as an invitation to repeat the offence.
