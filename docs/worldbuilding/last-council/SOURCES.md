# Historical and literary inspiration

> **Research checked 22 November 2026.** These are author notes, not player lore.
> [Index](README.md) · [Shared setting](SETTING.md)

The Houses, incidents and institutions in these drafts are original fiction.
Research supplies plausible sources of leverage: concealed debt, dependence on
foreign money, the sale of favour, competing privileges, misleading documents
and the gap between public reputation and private reality. We are combining
patterns from different periods, not recreating a particular historical state.

Institutional histories and museum/archive commentary are distinguished below
from literary fiction. No contemporary royal gossip or allegations about living
people are used. No modern adaptation's characters, script or text is copied.

## Research notes

| **R01 — Royal debt and political dependence** | The Royal Collection Trust describes the Prince of Wales's debts, creditor pressure and politically sensitive appeals for parliamentary relief. [Official correspondence of George IV as Prince of Wales](https://www.rct.uk/collection/royal-archives/georgian-papers-in-the-royal-archives/george-iv/official-correspondence-of-george-iv-as-prince-of-wales). | An impressive household may have little freedom to spend. A private liability can matter more than public wealth. Debt relief is a possible political bargain when the claim and available decision are fully supplied. |
| **R02 — A concealed family commitment** | The Royal Household's account records George's secret marriage to Maria Fitzherbert in 1785 and his later financial weakness. [George IV, 1820–1830](https://www.royal.uk/encyclopedia/george-iv-r-1820-1830). | Hidden commitments can clash with public expectations. We use disclosed fictional contractual facts rather than requiring players to reconstruct an unspecified relative's history or reproducing period religious discrimination. |
| **R03 — Forged access to royal favour** | Versailles recounts the 1784–1785 necklace fraud, including an impersonation and the use of supposed access to the Queen. It explicitly identifies Marie-Antoinette as innocent despite reputational damage. [The affair of the diamond necklace](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/key-dates/affair-diamond-necklace-1784-1785). | Supposed official authority can be traded, forged or misunderstood. An allegation is not automatically true. A player's private information must state what actually happened; a public complication must distinguish evidence from accusation. |
| **R04 — Provenance and uncertainty** | The V&A independently distinguishes the Queen's innocence from the unproven connection between the surviving Sutherland Diamonds and the affair. [Unboxing the Sutherland Diamonds](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-necklace-that-ruined-marie-antoinette-unboxing-the-sutherland-diamonds). | Separate a document's existence, its authenticity and the conclusions drawn from it. Avoid declaring a historical victim guilty or presenting uncertain evidence as an authoritative secret. |
| **R05 — An election financed by creditors** | The World of the Habsburgs explains the Fugger family's financing of Charles's 1519 election campaign and the resulting financial dependence. [Charles V and the dream of a universal monarchy](https://www.habsburger.net/en/chapter/charles-v-and-dream-universal-monarchy). | Support for a candidate can involve credit, guarantees and competing obligations. Our goals require negotiating actual terms during play; they do not start players in an undisclosed reciprocal alliance or force how someone votes. |
| **R06 — Taxation and consent** | UK Parliament's account describes Charles I's customs revenue, the forced loan of 1626 and the Petition of Right in 1628. [Charles I and the Petition of Right](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/civilwar/overview/petition-of-right/). | Rights, consent and exceptions make political questions beyond a simple rich-versus-poor split. A useful goal names the proposed rule and an attainable Council decision. Our fictional constitution is not a copy of the English one. |
| **R07 — Secret foreign subsidy** | UK Parliament's chronology identifies the secret Treaty of Dover in 1670 and French subsidies. [1660–1688 chronology](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/parliamentaryauthority/revolution/keydates/keydates1660-88/). | A family can depend on money from outside the realm while publicly asserting independence. Foreign trade remains lawful in the fictional setting; concealment and purchased political influence supply the conflict. |
| **R08 — Testing money rather than trusting appearances** | The Royal Mint Museum describes official metal standards and trial plates, including evidence of Tudor debasement. [Trial Plates](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/collection/trial-plates/). | Independent standards, audits and conflicting accounts can create Council dilemmas. A coinage-related incident is written evidence and policy, not a newly implemented currency or mint simulator. |
| **R09 — Manufactured public pressure, literary example** | In Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*, Cassius arranges letters intended to make Brutus believe opposition to Caesar has wider public backing. Folger's edition provides the play and scene summaries. [Julius Caesar](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/julius-caesar/read/), Act 1, scene 3 and Act 2, scene 1. | A petition's apparent breadth of support can conceal its origin. This is a literary device, not evidence for the historical event. The draft adapts persuasion and attribution without prescribing assassination or a predetermined ending. |
| **R10 — Inheritance and arbitrary authority, literary example** | In Shakespeare's *Richard II*, seizure of Gaunt's possessions denies Bolingbroke his inheritance and helps turn other nobles against the king. [Richard II, Folger edition](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/richard-ii/read/), synopsis and Act 2. | A dispute over one family's rights can make everyone consider the security of their own. We use arguable precedents and recorded policy rather than automatic property seizure, compulsory rebellion or a rightful scripted winner. |

## How to use the inspiration

- **Debt is a relationship, not a personality.** A debtor may be competent,
  generous, reckless or politically trapped. Different players can interpret
  the same starting circumstance differently.
- **Reputation can outlive its original basis.** A grain supplier may depend on
  imports; a supposedly poor House may hold valuable claims. Public identity
  provides expectations, while the selected private entries provide truth.
- **An institution creates several sides.** A monopoly can fund useful services
  while excluding competitors. Revocation can improve access while leaving an
  unpaid public obligation. Neither position needs a designated villain.
- **A secret must contain a playable fact.** “There is a troubling letter” is
  insufficient. State its relevant contents, authenticity and political stake.
  A physical copy is optional unless the author deliberately provides it.
- **A goal needs a playable situation.** If a pardon is ever added, include
  the offence, the argument for mercy and who has authority to consider it.
  Do not make an absent relative or an improvised trial the missing objective.
- **Complications create choices.** An outside petition or offer gives people
  something to debate; it does not dictate votes, secretly rewrite a House's
  starting facts or supply the Chancellor with the hidden cast.

## Source limits

The linked institutional web pages mostly have no displayed publication date;
the access/check date above bounds this research. Historical event dates are
given separately where relevant. Institutional narratives can simplify disputed
history, so precise sums, universal claims about noble behaviour and disputed
causal claims are deliberately avoided. The V&A comparison is used to preserve
the necklace affair's distinction between an actual fraud and an innocent
person's damaged reputation. Literary scenes are labelled as literary scenes.

The National Archives' Charles I educational page was discovered but its direct
web fetch returned 403 during this pass; it is not relied on as a fully inspected
source here. UK Parliament's accessible pages support the taxation/subsidy
patterns instead. All player-facing entries are newly written, without direct
quotations or historical persons inserted into the fictional kingdom.
